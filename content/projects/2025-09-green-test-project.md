## Composting the Interface is a photographic field guide to software after ownership. Jane Doe began the project by documenting devices that had left the commercial cycle but remained active in people’s lives: a router used as a local file server, a cracked tablet displaying recipes, an obsolete phone repurposed as a wildlife camera, and a monitor kept alive because its colour is still useful to a printmaker. The photographs resist the clean visual language of product launches. Cables are tangled, labels are handwritten, and repairs are visible.

Alongside the images, Jane collected stories about the decisions that make reuse possible. Who knows how to unlock an old device? Who has the time to search for a compatible part? Which operating systems can be trusted, and who is responsible when an unsupported machine becomes vulnerable? These questions shift attention from individual consumer choice to the infrastructures surrounding maintenance. Reuse is not simply a matter of being inventive. It depends on documentation, shared tools, accessible spaces, and collective agreements about risk.

The title refers to a form of decomposition that is social as much as material. Interfaces are designed to conceal the layers beneath them, while composting reveals that every object is made from dependencies. Jane photographed community workshops, gardens with improvised sensors, tool libraries, and rooms where old hardware is sorted for parts. The resulting images are paired with short annotations that name materials, software limitations, energy demands, and the people who contributed knowledge.

The project includes a printed guide with blank pages for readers to add their own observations. It deliberately avoids a universal recipe for reuse. A technique that works in one context may be unsafe or impossible in another. Instead, the guide proposes a slower methodology: identify the material history, ask who can maintain the system, document the compromise, and share the result. Composting the Interface treats technology as something that can return to common life through care, translation, and collective imagination rather than through novelty.
